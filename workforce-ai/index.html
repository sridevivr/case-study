Standing up the outbound GTM motion

When I joined WorkforceAI, there was no outbound motion in place. The GTM strategy was to begin outbound to mid-market companies in select sectors. My first job was to stand up the infrastructure that would let us actually send.

Going in, I didn't have a sales or GTM background. The first stretch was a crash course: YouTube videos, HubSpot and Apollo's documentation, and Reddit threads.

The work itself meant a series of tool and architecture choices, then an audit of what was already in place. I picked a free-tier stack that could grow with us: HubSpot as the system of record, Apollo for contact discovery, and Gmail with the HubSpot Sales Chrome extension as a sequencing workaround. I worked through the HubSpot import logic, companies first and then contacts linked to them, and kept the custom property layer deliberately lean. Only two fields beyond native HubSpot earned their place: Store Count and Expansion Status.

Building a personalized outreach system

The first targeted campaign was hand-curated. Every email had to read like the founder personally wrote it, which meant doing the research and the personalization work properly up front, not at scale.

I started by defining the ICP. Mid-market multi-site operators with around 20+ locations and under $500M in revenue are at the stage where labor planning starts breaking. They're past the size where intuition and spreadsheets work, but they can't afford McKinsey-grade consulting or Workday-level data infrastructure.

To turn the ICP into a list, I used Perplexity and Claude to surface around 110 operators across fitness, convenience, and apparel. The first pass identified candidates. The second pass researched expansion signals: new openings, hiring announcements, publicly stated expansion targets. Every claim came with a cited source so AI hallucinations could be spot-checked and discarded.

Then I ranked the 110 companies on an ordinal scale with stated criteria. Extremely clear expansion signals at the top, ambiguous in the middle, no news or contracting at the bottom. AI gives you a list. Forcing the rank turns the list into a usable shortlist. I picked the top five.

For those five companies, I pulled contacts from Apollo across the five functional clusters most likely to feel the product's value proposition: HR, retail operations, store operations, real estate, and finance. For each contact, I downloaded their LinkedIn profile as a PDF so the personalization data lived locally and didn't depend on any one platform staying accessible. After a verification pass, the final list was about 30 reachable contacts.

The deliverable was a self-contained HTML interface in the style of Clay.com. A sortable, filterable, searchable table of every contact, with a modal pop-up showing the full personalization detail. Five persona-bound templates compose into a personalized draft per contact at render time. A second page in the same file lets a non-technical user edit any template slot in a textarea with a live preview. Changes persist in the browser, with an export back to markdown.

Deliverable: contact + template interface

The last work was on voice. The first cut of templated drafts read slightly stiff, with a faintly consultant-y register. I gave granular feedback per template: eliminate consultant-speak, soften the credibility line, make the question low-stakes. The biggest structural change was dropping an auto-injected situation paragraph entirely. Drafts got shorter and more conversational. Three precision passes followed. Remove every em-dash, since they pattern-match to a slightly literary register. Ban contrastive framings, which read as pitch-shaped on a cold email. Tighten the recent-role-change opener so it only fires for people three months or less in seat, not anyone in the first 18 months.

Every email is grounded in real research, the templates compose cleanly, the voice has been tuned, and the surface is editable by anyone who needs to iterate on it later.
